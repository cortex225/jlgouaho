How do you become a developer in Quebec when you are starting from nothing, or close to it? I don't have a formula. I have a path, with dates and job titles, and a few lessons I wish someone had spelled out for me earlier.

This post is mainly for two kinds of readers. If you are coming from abroad, you are probably wondering where to start. If you work in retail or customer service, you may be wondering whether it's too late to switch.

---

## Where I started

Before I wrote code for a living, I was a sales specialist at Rogers in Granby. On paper, that has nothing to do with software. In practice, I spent my days listening to customers, figuring out what they actually needed, and offering something that made sense for them.

I did my DEC in computer science at Cégep de Granby and finished in May 2024. The program focused on software development, algorithms and web technologies.

One of my personal projects is Ma Civ, a plain HTML and CSS site that showcases Côte d'Ivoire, where my roots are. No framework, no database. I simply wanted to build something I could show people.

---

## The internship that opened the door

In August 2022, I joined Royal Broker Solutions as a full-stack developer intern. I built credit-value estimation sites in PHP and WordPress. Not glamorous, but it was real code used by real people.

About six months later, I was hired full time. I went on to become lead developer on InstaHR, the internal platform that manages contracts, employees and payments.

My takeaway: an internship is a trial period that runs both ways. The company sees how you work, and you find out whether you actually like the job. If you take it seriously, it can turn into an offer.

---

## Why the internship matters in a CEGEP computer science DEC

Technical computer science programs at CEGEP generally include a workplace internship (a "stage") near the end. Details vary between schools, so check the program page of the CEGEP you are looking at.

My advice is to treat that internship as your first job rather than one more course.

- Start looking early, and don't limit yourself to big companies. Smaller firms often have more room for an intern who wants to touch everything.
- Ask about the stack, how the team deploys, and who reviews the code.
- Keep notes on what you ship. Three months later, those notes become the bullet points on your resume.

When you are trying to land a first developer job in Quebec, a concrete internship often carries more weight than a long list of technologies.

## What sales and customer service already taught you

If you come from retail or customer service, you already have skills that many junior developers lack.

At Rogers, my job was to analyze customer needs and propose solutions that fit. That is the same exercise as gathering requirements from a user before writing a feature. I also built up my communication and negotiation skills there, and both come up constantly on a dev team.

At Royal Broker, part of my work was collaborating with HR, accounting and management to keep InstaHR aligned with what they needed. Those people don't think in APIs or database tables. I learned to listen, rephrase and say no politely at a sales counter, long before I needed it with stakeholders.

So don't hide those jobs on your resume or in interviews. Say what they taught you and connect it to the developer role.

## A portfolio of shipped projects, not tutorials

Recruiters see a lot of identical tutorial projects. What stands out is something with a real purpose that anyone can open and use.

My side projects vary a lot in scope. Ma Civ is a static site. MagicSearch is a Next.js app with PostgreSQL and the OpenAI API. More recently, SpinVibe and FitTrack are mobile apps built with React Native and Expo. Each one forced me to learn something I didn't know yet.

A few simple rules:

1. Put it online. A working link beats a GitHub repo with no README.
2. Write a short paragraph on the problem it solves and what was hard about it.
3. Two finished projects are worth more than six abandoned at 60%.

## Measurable results on your resume

"Took part in a cloud migration" doesn't say much. "Cut Azure costs from $600 to $135 a month (-78%)" tells the reader right away what you contributed.

That's what I did at Royal Broker, and I wrote up the details in [how I cut Azure costs by 78%](/en/blog/reduction-couts-azure-78-pourcent). Same idea for [the Python ATS that sorts 4,000+ resumes in seconds](/en/blog/ats-python-trier-4000-cvs): a number, an action, some context.

If you don't have a number, go find one. How long did a task take before and after? How many users? How many deploys per week? Even for a side project or an internship, there is almost always something you can measure.

## French and English in the Quebec job market

French is Quebec's official language, and it's the working language in many companies. If you are aiming for Granby, Sherbrooke or Quebec City, your French will matter a lot. In Montreal, plenty of teams work in both languages, and technical documentation is nearly always in English.

I won't throw made-up statistics at you. Work on both. If your French is strong, practice technical English by reading docs and writing your READMEs in English. If it's the other way around, take French courses and do your practice interviews in French.

## Immigration, studies and permits: use the official sources

I won't give you rules about study or work permits. They change, they depend on your situation, and a mistake can be costly.

If you are coming from abroad, start here:

- [Québec.ca](https://www.quebec.ca/en/immigration) for immigrating to Quebec, and its page on [studying in Quebec](https://www.quebec.ca/en/education/study-quebec).
- [Canada.ca, IRCC section](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship.html) for permits and federal applications.

Also check whether your CEGEP has an office for students arriving from outside Quebec, and ask them your questions directly.

## After the first job

Your first position is a starting point. After Royal Broker, I was a full-stack developer at Traction DK from August 2024 to January 2025, working on a SaaS CRM for car dealerships written in Groovy. Since March 2025, I've been an application developer at Evnia, an eco-taxation consulting firm in Montreal.

Three employers, three different stacks. Each time, being able to learn quickly mattered more than any specific technology on my resume. Right now I'm preparing for the Microsoft AZ-900 and AI-900 certifications.
